Benjamin Pikus

Research Scientist @ Luma AI • Previously Scale AI / founding eng @ Advex / Kitware / JHU

I lead RL for robotics at Luma AI. Our recent work, ZAP, turns a small open vision-language model into a robot policy trained entirely in simulation, with no real-robot data and no frontier teacher. ZAP matched GPT-6 Astra on real-world robots at 3× the speed and 1/17 the cost, and generalizes to new embodiments and tasks.

Before that, I was a core contributor to Uni-1, Luma’s unified model for image understanding and generation. I owned temporal reasoning and character consistency in pretraining and midtraining, from data through evaluation, teaching the model to keep characters and scenes consistent as a story moves through time. I was also responsible for RL for autoregressive and flow-matching generation, with a focus on legible text rendering.

Before Luma, I worked on data and evaluation for frontier labs’ post-training at Scale AI, and published on reward-model robustness under distribution shift and on vision-language model calibration. I was then the founding engineer at Advex AI, a synthetic-data startup for industrial vision, where I led R&D on the core product: diffusion and flow-matching models that generate labeled images in data-poor environments.

Across all of it, I’m interested in how models generalize beyond what they were trained on, and how what a model learns in one modality carries into another.

publications

  1. CoRL
    Pretraining Knows What, Simulation Teaches How: Compositional Generalization for Agentic Robotics
    Benjamin Pikus, Daniel Saunders, Mircea Mironenco, Salman Mohammadi, and 10 more authors
    In Conference on Robot Learning (CoRL), Late-Breaking Results, 2026
    Abs arXiv Bib Website

    Frontier models such as GPT-6 Astra now directly control robots and can generalize better than vision-language-action models (VLAs). We argue that this ability could arise from two distinct, composable skills: semantic reasoning, to understand what a task asks for in a scene, and motion control, to move the robot to do it. We show that small open VLMs are already capable of the first and mainly lack the second. We introduce Zero-real Agent Policy (ZAP), a recipe that teaches this missing motion skill, entirely in simulation on simple primitive tasks and from the model’s own successes, with no frontier teacher or distillation and no real-world robot data. ZAP reaches 96.7% success on real-world tasks, matching GPT-6 Astra in our harness while running 3x faster and 17x cheaper, and 74.0% on a bimanual adaptation of RoboLab-120, on par with GPT-6 Astra’s 72.2%. ZAP also generalizes to new objects, goals, and strategies, and in simulation to a robot embodiment on which it never trained.

  2. NeurIPS-W
    Hard Examples Are All You Need: Maximizing GRPO Post-Training Under Annotation Budgets
    Benjamin Pikus, Pratyush Ranjan Tiwari, and Burton Ye
    In NeurIPS 2025 Workshop on Efficient Reasoning, 2025
    Abs arXiv Bib

    Collecting high-quality training examples for language model fine-tuning is expensive, with practical budgets limiting the amount of data that can be procured. We investigate whether example difficulty affects GRPO training effectiveness by comparing selection strategies (easy, medium, hard, random) across multiple models and reasoning tasks. Training on the hardest 10% of examples (those where the base model fails most often) yields dramatic performance gains up to 47%, while easy examples produce minimal improvements of 3-15%. This occurs because GRPO requires outcome variance to generate learning signals; hard examples maintain mixed success/failure outcomes throughout training while easy examples quickly converge to consistent success, eliminating learning opportunities. Moreover, models trained on hard examples show superior out-of-distribution generalization, with only hard-trained models achieving meaningful gains on the AIME2025 benchmark. Our findings provide clear guidance: when budget-constrained, prioritize collecting and annotating examples where your base model struggles, as these drive nearly all learning value in GRPO fine-tuning.

  3. arXiv
    Understanding Trade offs When Conditioning Synthetic Data
    Brandon Trabucco, Qasim Wani, Benjamin Pikus, and Vasu Sharma
    arXiv preprint arXiv:2507.02217, 2025
    Abs arXiv Bib

    Learning robust object detectors from only a handful of images is a critical challenge in industrial vision systems, where collecting high quality training data can take months. Synthetic data has emerged as a key solution for data efficient visual inspection and pick and place robotics. Diffusion models promise a step change because they can generate high quality images in minutes, yet precise control, especially in low data regimes, remains difficult. We study eighty diverse visual concepts drawn from four standard object detection benchmarks and compare two conditioning strategies: prompt based and layout based. When the set of conditioning cues is narrow, prompt conditioning yields higher quality synthetic data; as diversity grows, layout conditioning becomes superior. When layout cues match the full training distribution, synthetic data raises mean average precision by an average of thirty four percent and by as much as one hundred seventy seven percent compared with using real data alone.

  4. NeurIPS-W
    Out-of-Distribution Detection & Applications With Ablated Learned Temperature Energy
    Will LeVine, Benjamin Pikus, Jacob Phillips, Berk Norman, and 2 more authors
    In NeurIPS 2024 Workshop on Bayesian Decision-making and Uncertainty, 2024
    Abs arXiv Bib

    We introduce Ablated Learned Temperature Energy (AbeT), an out-of-distribution detection method which lowers the False Positive Rate at 95% True Positive Rate (FPR@95) by 43.43% in classification compared to state of the art without training networks in multiple stages or requiring hyperparameters or test-time backward passes. We additionally provide empirical insights as to why our model learns to distinguish between in-distribution and OOD samples while only being explicitly trained on ID samples. Lastly, we show the efficacy of our method in identifying predicted bounding boxes and pixels corresponding to OOD objects in object detection and semantic segmentation, respectively.

  5. AAAI-W
    A Baseline Analysis of Reward Models’ Ability To Accurately Analyze Foundation Models Under Distribution Shift
    Will LeVine, Benjamin Pikus, Anthony Chen, and Sean Hendryx
    In AAAI 2024 Workshop, 2024
    Abs arXiv Bib

    Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback (RLHF) involves training a reward model to capture desired behaviors, which is then used to align LLMs. These reward models are additionally used at inference-time to estimate LLM responses’ adherence to those desired behaviors. However, there is little work measuring how robust these reward models are to distribution shifts. In this work, we evaluate how reward model performance, measured via accuracy and calibration, is affected by distribution shift. We show novel calibration patterns and accuracy drops due to OOD prompts and responses, and that the reward model is more sensitive to shifts in responses than prompts. Additionally, we adapt an OOD detection technique commonly used in classification to the reward model setting to detect these distribution shifts in prompts and responses.

  6. ICLR-W
    Enabling Calibration In The Zero-Shot Inference of Large Vision-Language Models
    Will LeVine, Benjamin Pikus, Pranav Raja, and Fernando Amat Gil
    In ICLR 2023 Workshop on Trustworthy ML, 2023
    Abs arXiv Bib

    Calibration of deep learning models is crucial to their trustworthiness and safe usage. However, there has yet to be a comprehensive study of the calibration of vision-language models that are used for zero-shot inference, like CLIP. We measure calibration across relevant variables like prompt, dataset, and architecture, and find that zero-shot inference with CLIP is miscalibrated. Furthermore, we propose a modified version of temperature scaling that is aligned with the common use cases of CLIP as a zero-shot inference model, and show that a single learned temperature generalizes for each specific CLIP model across inference dataset and prompt choice.
